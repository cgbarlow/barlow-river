# LinkedIn post: The River

Second draft, 18 November 2026. Storyline: the person who wrote it down. Ties the River to the "organisation as code" talk (Wellington Architects Forum), using the talk's own line that every family has a person who knows.

My grandfather spent years writing our family down. This week I handed it to my AI agents with one instruction: do something awesome with this.

Then I took my hands off the wheel.

I keep hearing the "bitter lesson" lately. Roughly: general methods and plenty of compute tend to beat our hand-built cleverness. So for fun I tried it on my own relatives.

What came back is the Barlow River. Four centuries of family drawn as streams that join at each marriage. Norwich weavers. A mathematician at Woolwich. New Zealand by the 1850s. A thread from Poland. I've had this tree since my twenties and never once seen it like this.

Here's what stuck with me. It only worked because somebody wrote it down. Every family has a person who knows. Ours was my grandfather, and he put it on paper.

Every organisation has that person too. Mostly, they haven't written it down. That's the whole idea behind the organisation as code: get what the place knows into a form that people and agents can both work from. Then "do something awesome with this" stops being a silly thing to say.

I still think there's no substitute for deep domain expertise. But now and then it pays to go full "conjuror", as Ethan Mollick puts it. Point the agents in a direction and let go.

## Other storylines the data would carry

1. **The crossing.** Fourteen people in the tree are recorded on both sides of the world. All fourteen went out to New Zealand. Not one is recorded coming back.
2. **The mathematician.** A line of Norwich worsted weavers produces Peter Barlow, born 1776, who ends up teaching mathematics at Woolwich. His son patents the tunnelling shield used under the Thames.
3. **Twelve Peter Barlows.** One name handed down for three hundred years, and what it takes to tell them apart.
4. **Two families, one year.** A couple married in Lambeth in 1874 and had a child in Christchurch the same year.
